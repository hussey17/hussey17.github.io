Before You Poll with LLMs: A Deliberative Diagnostic Framework

Ahmed Wali, Hassaan Tayyab

  • Computational Social Science
  • Human-Centred and Explainable AI

EMNLP 2026, Main Conference

Research conducted at IDSL, as part of the Topics in Large Language Models course, taught by Dr. Ihsan Ayyub Qazi (LUMS)

Paper Code Video
Details
  • Study design. We test dynamic fidelity: whether LLM personas change their opinions the way humans do after receiving new information. Using Stanford's America in One Room deliberative poll, we built 526 personas matched one-to-one to the human participants on the same 13 demographic attributes, including party, age, race, gender, education, income, state and LGBT identity. Each persona answered the poll's questions on a 0 to 10 scale (72 questions across immigration, healthcare, the economy, the environment and foreign policy). We then gave it the same balanced briefing materials the participants received and asked the same questions again, without showing it its earlier answers. Finally, we compared each persona's opinion shift with that of its human counterpart across five frontier models: GPT-5.1, Gemini 2.0 Flash, Claude Sonnet 4.5, Llama 3.3 70B and DeepSeek V3.
  • Key findings. No model matched human belief revision, and each failed in its own way. On questions about the opposing party, where humans became less hostile after deliberation, GPT-5.1 personas became more hostile (reversal). Gemini, Claude and Llama shifted in the right direction but at 5 to 7 times the human magnitude (overshoot). DeepSeek barely moved (rigidity). GPT-5.1 reversed on 80% of these outgroup questions but only 26% of policy questions. Two ablations isolated the cause. Replacing the policy briefings with irrelevant Wikipedia articles removed GPT-5.1's reversal, showing that policy content triggers it. Naming the target party explicitly produced opposite failures in the same model: GPT-5.1 reversed on outgroup questions and overshot on ingroup ones, while Gemini showed the inverse.
  • Self-sycophancy and implications. We term this pattern self-sycophancy: a model conforms to its own stereotype of the persona instead of reasoning from the information it is given. GPT-5.1 follows a stereotype of outgroup hostility, and Gemini one of ingroup favoritism. Static evaluations, which only check whether a persona holds the right opinion at one point in time, cannot detect these failures. Used for policy research, GPT-5.1 would suggest that deliberation increases hostility, Gemini would overstate its effect, and DeepSeek would suggest it has none. For researchers and practitioners, we propose a four-step protocol to run before trusting LLM personas to simulate opinion change.

Promises and Perils of Public Leaderboards for Model Selection

Hassaan Tayyab, Haris Zafar, Ayaan Zia, Shamsa Abid, Abdul Ali Bangash

  • AI for Software Engineering
  • Empirical Software Engineering

MSR 2027, Technical Track

Supervised by Dr. Abdul Ali Bangash (LUMS) and Dr. Shamsa Abid (Postdoc, SMU)

Details
  • Study design. We mined GitHub, Hugging Face and the official HELM catalog for 449,258 leaderboard candidates. Two layers of automated filtering reduced these to 13,765, and manual screening, calibrated by three authors on a 10% stratified sample (pairwise Cohen's κ 0.73 to 0.86), produced a corpus of 1,555 foundation-model leaderboards. Three authors verified LLM-assisted extractions and coded what each leaderboard discloses into a shared codebook, then derived the promises and perils of that information for model selection. We built five model-selection scenarios from these and surveyed 17 developers across three software development firms.
  • Key findings. Leaderboards disclose seven categories of information. Tasks and datasets are the most common (77.4%), followed by score breakdowns (58.0%), result provenance (57.3%), evaluation code (53.5%), evaluation settings (41.0%) and versioning (40.3%). Cost and resource use is the rarest (15.8%). From these we derive five promises, such as shortlisting models tested on relevant tasks, and five perils, such as scores that do not transfer across evaluation setups and aggregate scores that hide task-specific weaknesses. Developers judged models by the total cost of usable output, including retries, manual fixes and review time, rather than by price alone.
  • Implications. We combine the corpus analysis and developer responses into five guidelines: check task and setup compatibility, compare the total cost of usable output, validate reported advantages on project-relevant tasks, scale verification to the cost of failure, and reassess whether model updates justify a switch.

The Impact of Generative AI on Open Source Software Development: A Systematic Review and Mapping Study

Hassaan Tayyab, Sehrish Habib, Aaliyah Chang, Mariam Guizani

  • AI for Software Engineering
  • Empirical Software Engineering

ACM Transactions on Software Engineering and Methodology (TOSEM)

Supervised by Dr. Mariam Guizani (Queen's University)

Details
  • Study design. Following Kitchenham and Charters' guidelines, we searched the ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, Scopus and Web of Science through their APIs for peer-reviewed studies published from January 2023 to June 2026, using a query validated against 13 control papers. Of 10,530 records, 7,556 remained after removing duplicates and records without verifiable DOIs. Three authors independently screened titles and abstracts, jointly reviewed 110 shortlisted records and screened 18 full texts, which yielded 12 studies. Backward and forward snowballing added 16 more, for a final corpus of 28. We characterised each study's tools, OSS settings and methods, then mapped its reported outcomes onto Feng et al.'s socio-technical roadmap, which applies McLuhan's tetrad to 67 scenarios across Tasks, Technology, People and Structures.
  • Key findings. The evidence is recent and concentrated: 16 of the 28 studies appeared in 2026, 15 were published at MSR, and 25 are sample studies of software artifacts that often reuse the AIDev and DevGPT datasets. GitHub Copilot (17 studies) and ChatGPT (13) are the most studied tools. Of the 67 scenarios, 18 are supported, one has mixed evidence, none are contradicted and 48 (71.6%) have not been studied. The mixed case is code quality, which improves in some studies and deteriorates in others. Support is concentrated in Tasks and Technology (17 of 19 scenarios), while only two People scenarios and none of the 11 Structures scenarios have supporting evidence.
  • Implications. Studies document GenAI supporting test generation, routine maintenance and multilingual documentation, but evidence on review effort, contributor learning and retention, and the long-term cost of maintaining generated code remains limited. We recommend that contributors verify generated changes, that maintainers set explicit disclosure and verification rules and account for review and maintenance effort, and that researchers run longitudinal studies linking repository data with direct measures of contributor and maintainer experience.

Human–AI Collaboration in Ophthalmic Diagnosis: A Randomized Controlled Trial of Multimodal AI Assistance and Automation Bias

Abdul Rafay Bajwa, Hassaan Tayyab, Muhammad Ibrahim Anwar, Ihsan Ayyub Qazi, Ayesha Ali, Hamad Alizai, Yanda Meng

  • Human-Centred and Explainable AI

Graduation Thesis

Supervised by Dr. Ihsan Ayyub Qazi (LUMS), in collaboration with Dr. Yanda Meng (KAUST)

Details

This randomized controlled trial tests how physicians work with a multimodal AI system when diagnosing retinal disease. Twenty resident physicians first complete an AI-literacy training module and are then randomized to diagnose either with a multimodal AI assistant, which combines a vision-language model with a text-based reasoning model, or with conventional diagnostic resources alone. Each participant works through 17 locally sourced retinal fundus images. Together they cover 17 diagnoses, including stages of diabetic retinopathy, retinal vein and artery occlusions, age-related macular degeneration and retinitis pigmentosa, along with 34 additional differential diagnoses. A panel of three senior ophthalmology consultants evaluates the participants' diagnoses and reasoning. The trial asks whether AI assistance improves diagnostic accuracy, and how physicians respond when the AI is wrong. Calibrated uncertainty signals may help them recognise and override an incorrect recommendation, or they may still defer to the AI, increasing automation bias. The aim is to inform safer human-AI diagnostic workflows.

Depends on What? An Empirical Study of Dependency Injection in FastAPI

Zain Ahmed Khan, Yahya Ahmad, Hassaan Tayyab, Shamsa Abid, Abdul Ali Bangash

  • Empirical Software Engineering

To be submitted to EASE 2027, Technical Track

Supervised by Dr. Abdul Ali Bangash (LUMS) and Dr. Shamsa Abid (Postdoc, SMU)

Details

FastAPI ships with a built-in dependency injection (DI) system, yet little is known about how developers wire dependencies in practice or what that wiring costs at runtime. We mine about 5,000 candidate FastAPI repositories from GitHub and use static analysis to classify each application into one of four DI strategies: FastAPI's native DI, an external DI container, manual DI or no DI, along with the constructs and libraries used within each. We then extract structural properties of the dependency wiring for every endpoint, such as the number of dependencies it reaches, synchronous providers on its request path, chain depth and dependency lifecycle. Finally, we test whether these properties predict runtime cost: we calibrate a cost model on controlled applications, pre-register predicted rankings of routes in real applications, and compare them with measured dependency-resolution times. The goal is evidence on which DI patterns carry runtime cost, and a static estimate that developers and tools can use without load testing.
